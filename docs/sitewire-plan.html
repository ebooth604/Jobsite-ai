Initial business plan · Vancouver BC · Canadian market · working title

SitewireJobsite AI that counts what the crew installed — and proves what it wasn't paid for.

Production intelligence for Canadian specialty subcontractors. Every funded jobsite-AI company sells visibility into the sub's work, to the general contractor. Nobody sells it to the sub — the party that carries the labour risk, eats the rework, and loses the change-order argument for lack of evidence. Canada has spent seven years building a statutory regime that decides those arguments in weeks, and it rewards whoever has the documentation.

01Thesis

Subs measure hours precisely and installed quantity not at all

A specialty sub bids a job as quantity × labour productivity: N linear feet of conduit at X hours per hundred. Everything after the bid is a race between what was assumed and what actually happens. The sub tracks one side of that ratio to the penny — payroll — and the other side not at all.

So a job that's going to lose 400 hours announces itself in week three and is discovered in week eight, when the recoverable window has already closed. And when the money argument starts, the GC's version of site conditions is better-evidenced than the sub's.

Sitewire turns the photos crews already take into installed-quantity tracking, joins it to their labour hours and bid rates, and assembles the evidence package that gets the out-of-scope work paid for.
02Why Canada

The most adjudication-dense construction market in the world

Province by province, Canada has built statutory prompt payment and interim adjudication — payment disputes resolved in weeks instead of years. It pays whoever walks in with dated, contemporaneous documentation, and punishes the firm reconstructing its case from memory. Most subcontractors still keep that evidence in a text thread.

Three consequences that shape the plan

  1. The pain is predictable, because it already happened. We don't have to speculate about what BC subs will need — Ontario and Alberta have been living it for years. Ontario adjudication practice is a free specification for our BC product.
  2. BC is the best beachhead; Ontario is the best market. BC's regime is new, so attention is high and behaviour is unformed — the moment a sub is most willing to change how it documents. Ontario has four times the ICP firms. Land where the urgency is, expand to where the volume is.
  3. This is a durable need, not a launch window. Adjudication isn't going away in any province that adopted it. That's a decade of relevance, not a two-year news cycle.
The counterweight, stated plainly: adjudication is our sharpest "why now" and our sharpest single point of failure. If BC uptake disappoints, the evidence package reverts to an ordinary change-order tool. Survivable — the productivity half stands alone — but it costs us the wedge. Two lawyer conversations in the first 60 days test it cheaply.

04Product

One loop, five steps

No new hardware. No 360° rig, no drone, no BIM model — deliberate constraints, because our buyer has none of those things and our competitors all require at least one.

01CaptureCrews shoot photos and 30-second walk videos on the phone in their pocket — or we ingest the photo stream already flowing into Procore or Autodesk Build.
02ExtractVision models estimate installed quantities against the bid's scope items, locate them by area, and flag conditions worth remembering: blocked access, stacked trades, unfinished predecessor work, damage.
03ReconcileInstalled quantity joins that day's labour hours and the bid's budgeted units-per-hour. Output: a productivity factor per scope item, per crew, per day.
04Alert"Level 2 branch conduit has run at 0.71 of bid productivity for six working days. At this rate the scope finishes 320 hours over. Top correlated condition: ceiling grid installed ahead of rough-in in 4 of 6 areas."
05DocumentWhen a condition is billable, the system assembles a dated, geolocated, photo-backed package in the shape a change order, a notice of non-payment response, or an adjudication submission actually needs.

Step 5 is what gets us renewed. Steps 3–4 are what make us hard to replace.

05Privacy

Worker privacy is a product decision, not a legal afterthought

Jobsite photos contain workers' faces — personal information under BC's PIPA, PIPEDA federally, and Quebec's stricter Law 25. Canadian construction is also heavily unionized. A product that reads to the building trades as surveillance is dead on arrival regardless of its legal footing, and deservedly so.

Committed in the product and in the contract
  • We measure installed work, not workers. No individual productivity metric exists in the data model — not hidden, not gated, absent. The unit of analysis is the scope item and the crew, never the person.
  • Faces blurred at ingest, before storage, original discarded.
  • Canadian data residency, Canadian cloud regions, full stop.
  • A plain-language worker notice the customer can post on site.

In a Canada-only business this stops being a compliance cost and becomes a commercial asset. Much of ICI work is public or quasi-public — health authorities, school districts, universities, BC Hydro, TransLink, Infrastructure Ontario — and those owners' privacy and residency questions flow downhill to their subs. An all-Canadian, Canadian-owned, no-individual-metrics answer is one a US-headquartered competitor cannot give without qualification.

06Consequence

What Canada-only does to the shape of the company

A ~3,200-firm national ICP means we cannot grow on new-logo velocity. At any believable win rate, logo count runs out. The company has to grow the other way — and that is a design constraint on the product, not just the sales plan.

  • Expansion revenue is the primary engine. Net revenue retention above 120% isn't a nice metric here, it's the growth model. That pulls multi-trade support and per-project expansion earlier in the roadmap than a US plan would.
  • Churn is disproportionately expensive. Every lost account is also a lost reference in a community where everyone knows everyone.
  • Depth per account beats breadth of product. Better to be indispensable to one electrical contractor across all 30 of its jobs than adequate to six trades on one job each.
  • A smaller sales team, hired later. Founder-led selling can plausibly cover the Lower Mainland and much of Alberta. The first AEs are for Ontario.
  • The exit is Canadian-shaped. At ~$36M ARR against a $256M SAM this is a strategic acquisition — Procore, Autodesk, Trimble, a Canadian consolidator — not an IPO. A legitimate outcome that should be said out loud to investors rather than discovered by them.

If the constraint later relaxes, the same product sells into a US market roughly six times larger. Nothing in this plan depends on that, and no milestone assumes it.

07Market

08Competition

Why we're not roadkill

The reality-capture category is well capitalized and largely won at the GC and owner tier — Buildots raised a $45M Series D, Trunk Tools $30.5M, Fyld $41M. We are not attacking it. We're arguing the sub is a separate market: separate buyer, separate data (labour hours and bid units, which the GC never sees), separate value event.

Canada-only buys a real defensive benefit: we are too small a prize, in too specific a regulatory context, to be worth a US incumbent's dedicated attention for several years. That isn't a moat — but it is time, and time is what the data moat needs.

Defensibility, ranked by how much we actually believe it

  1. Data. Installed quantity ↔ labour hours ↔ bid rate, by trade, across firms. Nobody else has both sides of that ratio, because nobody else sells to the party that owns both.
  2. Regulatory fit. Evidence packages shaped to Canadian adjudication practice, province by province. Localization a global product treats as an edge case is our core.
  3. Workflow lock. Once change orders are documented in our format and estimators tune bid rates against our history, we're inside the annual planning cycle.
  4. Trust in the trade. Compounds fast in a small referral market — and works against us in each new province, where we start unknown.
  5. Privacy and residency. Hard for a GC-facing or US-headquartered competitor to match without qualification.
  6. Model quality. Real, but a depreciating asset. We should not tell ourselves this is the moat.

10Pricing

Per active project, not per seat

Seat pricing punishes the customer for putting more crew on capture — exactly the behaviour our data quality depends on. Project pricing scales with delivered value and matches how a sub already thinks about cost coding.

TierPriceIncludes
Pilot$16K / 90 daysUp to 4 projects, accuracy report, exit review — refundable against year one
Core$475 / proj / moProduction tracking, daily reports, alerts, one trade model
Plus$700 / proj / mo+ evidence packages (change order and adjudication), multi-trade, bid-rate feedback, API

Pricing discipline matters more in a small market. With a capped logo count, ACV growth per account is the growth model. Build the expansion path into the first contract: pricing that makes adding the fifth, tenth and twentieth project obviously worth it, and a Plus tier the customer grows into rather than negotiates for.

Explicitly not doing

Contingency pricing on recovered change orders and adjudication awards. It's the pricing the value story begs for, and it turns us into a claims-consulting firm with software attached: unsellable multiple, adversarial relationship with the GC ecosystem, and possibly near the line on providing legal services. Revisit only with counsel.

11Capital

The Vancouver base and the Canadian capital stack

Cash-efficient R&D

As a CCPC: 35% federal refundable SR&ED credit — enhanced to a $6M expenditure limit — plus BC's 10% refundable credit. A large fraction of our biggest cost line returns as cash, not a deduction.

The rest of the non-dilutive stack

NRC IRAP for the technical build. Mitacs Accelerate for UBC/SFU graduate ML interns at a fraction of loaded cost — unusually well matched to a vision problem with a real industrial partner.

Angel economics that favour our best investors

BC's Investment Capital Program gives eligible BC investors a 30% refundable credit on investments in registered Eligible Business Corporations. Our ideal angels are Lower Mainland contractors who feel this problem viscerally — and this makes their cheque materially cheaper. Register as an EBC early.

Talent and proximity

Deep CV bench from UBC/SFU and the local VFX and games industry, well below Bay Area cost. And a founder can stand in four jobsite trailers in a day.

Cost: thinner pre-seed capital

Canada's early-stage market is smaller and more syndicate-driven. Construction-industry angels first, BDC and Canadian pre-seed funds as follow-on — a longer raise than a comparable US company.

Cost: no currency arbitrage

CAD costs against CAD revenue. The uplift a cross-border plan would have had is gone, which makes SR&ED recovery and pricing discipline matter more, not less.

15Open

Questions we haven't answered

  1. Trade choice. Electrical + concrete forming is a guess tuned to this market. Mechanical piping may beat both in BC's institutional pipeline. Decide with discovery data, not preference.
  2. Alberta or Ontario second? Alberta is closer, cheaper to enter and has live adjudication; Ontario has four times the firms and the mature market. The plan says Alberta on cost-of-entry grounds — but if Ontario discovery shows the adjudication channel already sells itself there, that ordering should flip.
  3. How hard do we lean on adjudication? Sharpest "why now" and sharpest single point of failure. §14 tests it directly in the first 60 days.
  4. Do we need the bid takeoff? The ratio needs budgeted units. If subs won't share takeoffs early, we need a cold-start path — crew-relative trending instead of bid-relative.
  5. What's the honest ceiling, and who should fund it? A $256M SAM supports a strong, durable, profitable Canadian software business and a strategic exit. It does not support a fund-returning outcome for a large VC. Pick investors accordingly.
  6. Where does safety fit? Easiest to detect, hardest to charge a sub for, and it collides with our privacy stance. Probably never the wedge.
